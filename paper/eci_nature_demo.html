Exploratory Causal Inference × Behavioural Science

Riccardo Cadei Draft of 16 Oct 2026 Results are placeholders until validated
Abstract

Randomized experiments are how behavioural science establishes cause and effect, yet what an experiment can reveal is fixed before it starts: researchers decide which behaviours to score, and any effect outside that list goes unseen. Video records far more than anyone annotates. Here we introduce do-pilot, an empiricist assistant that reads the raw videos of a randomized experiment and proposes which behaviours the treatment changed, before anyone decides what to look for. do-pilot learns, without labels, a dictionary of interpretable visual units (neurons) that each fire on one recognisable pattern of behaviour, and searches it with an interventional contrast that returns the smallest set of neurons carrying everything the treatment changed, with statistical error control. The scientist stays in the loop throughout: behaviours they already score can be given to the search so that it reports only what is new, their own hypotheses are tested alongside the tool's, and every proposal is named, interpreted and validated by people, with a language model as an aid. Applied to three systems, fungal exposure in ant colonies, autism-risk genes in mice and motor-neuron transcription factors in Xenopus frogs, do-pilot proposes novel hypotheses in each, which we validate with targeted follow-up annotation and experiments, yielding new insights into social immunity, social behaviour and motor development.

TL;DR

A behavioural experiment only finds effects on the behaviours someone chose to score. Here the scientist and an AI system choose together. The videos propose what the treatment changed; the scientist's own knowledge can enter before the search, as behaviours already scored, or after it, as hypotheses of their own; and every candidate is named and tested by people. In ants, mice and frogs this produced new, validated findings.

1Motivation

Behavioural experiments measure the effect of a treatment on a short list of behaviours chosen in advance. That list is the bottleneck: it reflects what the field already expects, and whatever else the treatment changed stays in the footage, unseen. AI annotators have made scoring faster, but they still answer only questions someone already asked.

We build an empiricist assistant for the step before: choosing what to measure. It proposes; the scientist decides. To be trusted it must be label-free (the expected result cannot shape what it sees), readable (every proposal can be watched and named) and statistically controlled (the list is not a list of noise).

2do-pilot

The five steps of Fig. 1. Steps 2 and 3 are automatic and identical for every species and lab.

2.1Experiment

Step 1 · Scientist

Videos from a randomized experiment with a fixed camera, and the treatment of each animal or group. Behaviours the lab already scores are optional; if given, they become extra units of the representation, so the search reports only what changed beyond them.

2.2Behavioural representation

Step 2 · do-pilot

A frozen vision model encodes each frame; the tokens on the animals, each with its recent change, pass through a sparse dictionary (sparse autoencoder) whose neurons each fire on one visual pattern. Every video is summarised per neuron by time active, bouts per minute and latency to the first bout.

Priors. The representation is shaped only by assumptions that hold in every domain: a fixed viewpoint, sparsity (few behaviours at once), position and rotation invariance, and recurrence (behaviour repeats). Labels never shape it.

How choices are made. Every prior and implementation choice is treated as a hypothesis and tested on public, expert-annotated behaviour datasets (MABe22: mice, beetles and ants; CalMS21: mouse pairs). These are not controlled experiments, but their labels tell us whether neurons match behaviour. The score is alignment: for each annotated behaviour, the best single neuron is picked on half the videos and its precision measured on the other half. A choice is kept only if alignment rises across datasets. Our own experiments are never used for tuning; they get one pre-registered run of the frozen recipe.

2.3Interventional contrast

Step 3 · do-pilot

Neurons are correlated, so one real effect lights up many neurons that only co-vary with it. We want the few that carry the whole effect.

Definition · Neural Effect Sufficiency Search, NESS(ε)

Let T be the randomized treatment and Z ∈ ℝm a video's neuron summaries. A set S ⊆ {1,…,m} is ε-sufficient if

I( T ; Z−S | ZS ) ≤ ε

NESS(ε) returns an inclusion-minimal ε-sufficient set: a treatment classifier that sees only ZS loses at most ε nats of log-loss against one that sees every neuron.

Because T is randomized, any behavioural change it causes passes through ZS, up to an error that shrinks with ε bound to be verified. It assumes only independent videos, a dictionary fixed before the analysis, and sample splitting. Neurons are added by held-out information gain, redundant ones removed, and the final set certified on held-out data.

Why not NES. Our earlier Neural Effect Search has no defined target, orders neurons by raw effect size under an assumption that fails in general, and never removes a neuron once selected, so its precision falls as data grow.

2.4Interpretation

Step 4 · Scientist + AI

a · Neural interpretation: what does the neuron show? The scientist watches the clips where the neuron fires most, and a random sample where it fires at all, from both arms. A vision-language model drafts a short name and description from the same clips; the scientist accepts, edits or rejects it. NESS also says whether the neuron is an effect (it shifts with the treatment) or context (needed only to read another neuron).

b · Biological interpretation: why would the treatment change it? The named behaviour becomes a hypothesis about mechanism. A language model searches the literature for known links between the treatment and the behaviour and drafts candidate explanations; the scientist judges them and states a testable prediction. The scientist's own hypotheses join the list here.

2.5Validation

Step 5 · Scientist

Each interpretation gets its own check, on data the search never saw. The name must be checked first: an effect on a named behaviour only means something if the neuron really shows that behaviour. The biological mechanism itself is tested by the next experiment.

Name checkBlind raters see top and random clips from both arms and mark whether the named behaviour is present.checks 4a
Nuisance checkThe neuron's effect is tested against recording day, camera and batch.guards 4a and 4b
