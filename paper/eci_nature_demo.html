Exploratory Causal Inference × Behavioural Science

Riccardo Cadei Draft of 16 Oct 2026 Results are placeholders until validated
Abstract

Randomized experiments are how behavioural science establishes cause and effect, yet what an experiment can reveal is fixed before it starts: researchers decide which behaviours to score, and any effect outside that list goes unseen. Video records far more than anyone annotates. Here we introduce do-pilot, an empiricist assistant that reads the raw videos of a randomized experiment and proposes which behaviours the treatment changed, before anyone decides what to look for. do-pilot learns, without labels, a dictionary of interpretable visual units (neurons) that each fire on one recognisable pattern of behaviour, and searches it with an interventional contrast that returns the smallest set of neurons carrying everything the treatment changed, with statistical error control. The scientist stays in the loop throughout: behaviours they already score can be given to the search so that it reports only what is new, their own hypotheses are tested alongside the tool's, and every proposal is named, interpreted and validated by people, with AI models as aids. Applied to three systems, fungal exposure in ant colonies, autism-risk genes in mice and motor-neuron transcription factors in Xenopus frogs, do-pilot proposes novel hypotheses in each, which we validate with held-out annotation and independent cohorts, yielding new insights into social immunity, social behaviour and motor development.

TL;DR

Experiments find only the effects someone thought to score. Here the videos propose what changed, the scientist adds what they already know, and people name and test every candidate: new, validated findings in ants, mice and frogs.

1Motivation

“How odd it is that anyone should not see that all observation must be for or against some view if it is to be of any service!”

Charles Darwin, letter to Henry Fawcett, 18 September 1861

Behavioural experiments follow Darwin's rule: the view comes first. Before filming, the researcher fixes a short list of behaviours to score (an ethogram), and the experiment can only confirm or reject effects on that list. Whatever else the treatment changed stays in the footage, unseen. AI annotators have made scoring faster, but they still answer only the questions already asked.

Everything the video records
The ethogram: behaviours chosen in advance

Brown: effects an experiment can test today. Blue: effects of the treatment that nobody scored. do-pilot searches the whole field.

We build an empiricist assistant that lets the data suggest the view. It proposes; the scientist decides. To be trusted it must be label-free (the expected result cannot shape what it sees), readable (every proposal can be watched and named) and statistically controlled (the list is not a list of noise).

2do-pilot

The five steps of Fig. 1. Steps 2 and 3 are automatic and identical for every species and lab.

2.1Experiment

Step 1 · Scientist

We focus on randomized experiments in the behavioural sciences: a gene is mutated, a colony is exposed to a pathogen, an odour or a drug is given, and the animals are filmed. These experiments are common across ecology, neuroscience and developmental biology, and their videos are usually analysed only for a few predefined behaviours. The input to do-pilot is exactly what such a lab already has: videos from a fixed camera and the treatment of each animal or group.

2.2Behavioural representation

Step 2 · do-pilot

A frozen vision model encodes each frame. The tokens on the animals, each paired with its recent change, pass through a sparse dictionary (sparse autoencoder) whose neurons each fire on one visual pattern. Every video is summarised per neuron by time active, bouts per minute and latency to the first bout. Labels never shape the representation. The only assumptions are these priors:

Fixed viewpoint
One camera, one arena: a change in the image is a change in the animals.
Behaviour lives on the animals
What matters is the animals' appearance and how it changes over a few seconds.
Position invariance
A behaviour means the same thing anywhere in the arena.
Rotation invariance
…and in any orientation.
Sparsity
At any moment, only a few behaviours are present.
Heterogeneous recurrence rate
A behaviour recurs, and how often it recurs differs between animals and videos. That variation is what a treatment can change.

Prior knowledge, a priori. If the lab already scores some behaviours, the scores can be appended as extra units. They are hypotheses too, and the search in §2.3 treats them like any neuron: a scored behaviour is kept if it carries part of the treatment's effect and discarded if it does not. When kept, the neurons selected beside it show what the treatment changed beyond it.

How choices are made

Every prior and implementation choice is treated as a hypothesis and tested on public, expert-annotated behaviour datasets. These are not controlled experiments, but their labels tell us whether neurons match behaviour. The score is alignment: for each annotated behaviour, the best single neuron is picked on half the videos and its precision measured on the other half. A choice is kept only if alignment rises across datasets. Our own experiments are never used for tuning; they get one pre-registered run of the frozen recipe.

2.3Interventional contrast identification

Step 3 · do-pilot

Neurons are correlated, so one real effect lights up many neurons that only co-vary with it. We identify the few that carry the whole effect, in two tiers: a statistical target that needs only randomization, and a causal refinement that needs one further assumption.

Tier 1 · Neural Effect Sufficiency Search, NESS(ε)

Let T be the randomized treatment and Z ∈ ℝm a video's neuron summaries. A set S ⊆ {1,…,m} is ε-sufficient if

I( T ; Z−S | ZS ) ≤ ε

NESS(ε) returns an inclusion-minimal ε-sufficient set: a treatment classifier that sees only ZS loses at most ε nats of log-loss against one that sees every neuron. Because T is randomized, any behavioural change it causes passes through ZS, up to an error that shrinks with ε bound to be verified. Assumes only independent videos, a dictionary fixed beforehand, and sample splitting.

Tier 2 · Direct effects, with one causal assumption

NESS can include context neurons that the treatment does not change but that are needed to read another neuron's change. To separate them, assume that whatever must be held fixed is itself in the dictionary: neurons capture the relevant behavioural factors closely, and no hidden cause links two affected factors. Then a selected neuron is a direct effect if it stays dependent on T given every subset of the other selected neurons. A neuron that almost perfectly reveals the treatment hides the others; they are reported as undetermined, never as absent.

Comparison with NES

Our earlier method, Neural Effect Search (NES), fails on four counts:

  • No defined target. It never states which set of neurons it is trying to recover, and its additivity assumption rules out mediated effects.
  • Ordering by raw effect size. It picks the neuron with the largest change first, which depends on each neuron's scale; its claim that this neuron always belongs to the true set is false.
  • No backward step. A neuron, once selected, is never removed, so redundant neurons accumulate and precision falls as data grow.
  • Error control. Its claim that the family-wise error rate goes to zero does not hold at a fixed test level.

2.4Interpretation

Step 4 · Scientist + AI

a · Neural interpretation: what does the neuron show? The scientist watches the clips where the neuron fires most, and a random sample where it fires at all, from both arms. A vision-language model drafts a short name and description from the same clips; the scientist accepts, edits or rejects it.

b · Biological interpretation: why would the treatment change it? The named behaviour becomes a hypothesis about mechanism. A language model searches the literature for known links between the treatment and the behaviour and drafts candidate explanations; the scientist judges them and states a testable prediction. The scientist's own hypotheses join the list here.

2.5Validation

Step 5 · Scientist + ML

A new experiment is the strongest test of a hypothesis, but it is slow and often not possible. Validation therefore uses data that already exist but that the search never saw. The name is checked first, because an effect on a named behaviour only means something if the neuron really shows that behaviour.

Scoring the named behaviour by hand on every unseen video would be slow, so the scientist labels a small sample and an AI annotator, trained on those labels, scores the rest. Prediction-powered causal inference (PPCI) combines the two so that the effect estimate and its confidence interval stay valid even though the annotator makes mistakes.

Name checkBlind raters see top and random clips from both arms and mark whether the named behaviour is present.checks 4a
Nuisance checkThe effect is tested against recording day, camera and batch.guards 4a and 4b
Effect checkThe search runs on half the videos. On the other half, the named behaviour is scored blind to treatment (a few by hand, the rest by an AI annotator) and its effect estimated with PPCI in a pre-registered test.checks 4b
ReplicationThe same effect in an independent cohort already filmed: another experiment series, genetic line or species.checks 4b, generality
When a collaborating lab can run a targeted follow-up experiment, it is the final test of the mechanism, and its videos go back into step 1. It is not required for a validated hypothesis.
