Exploratory Causal Inference × Behavioural Science

Riccardo Cadei Draft of 16 Oct 2026 Results are placeholders until validated
Abstract

Randomized experiments are how behavioural science establishes cause and effect, yet what an experiment can reveal is fixed before it starts: researchers decide which behaviours to score, and any effect outside that list goes unseen. Video records far more than anyone annotates. Here we introduce do-pilot, an empiricist assistant that reads the raw videos of a randomized experiment and proposes which behaviours the treatment changed, before anyone decides what to look for. do-pilot learns, without labels, a dictionary of interpretable visual units (neurons) that each fire on one recognisable pattern of behaviour, and searches it with an interventional contrast that returns the smallest set of neurons carrying everything the treatment changed, with statistical error control. Each selected neuron is a hypothesis that a scientist can watch, name and test. Applied to three systems, fungal exposure in ant colonies, autism-risk genes in mice and motor-neuron transcription factors in Xenopus frogs, do-pilot proposes novel hypotheses in each, which we validate with targeted follow-up annotation and experiments, yielding new insights into social immunity, social behaviour and motor development.

TL;DR

A behavioural experiment can only find effects on the behaviours someone chose to score. We let the videos propose what to score: the treatment's effect is searched over a label-free representation of behaviour, and what it returns becomes a hypothesis to test. In ants, mice and frogs this produced new, validated findings.

1Motivation

Behavioural science runs on randomized experiments. A gene is knocked out, a colony is exposed to a pathogen, and the animals are filmed. The effect is then measured on a short list of behaviours chosen in advance (an ethogram). That list is the bottleneck: it is fixed before the data exist, it reflects what the field already expects, and scoring it by hand takes hours per hour of video. Whatever else the treatment changed stays in the footage, unseen.

Machine learning has so far sped up the scoring. AI annotators label known behaviours, and prediction-powered causal inference keeps their estimates valid. Both still answer only questions someone already asked. What is missing is help with choosing the questions.

We build an empiricist assistant for that step. It does not theorise or decide what is true. It reads an experiment's videos and proposes a short list of observable changes the treatment caused. The scientist names, tests and interprets them. To be trusted, it must be:

  • Label-free: nothing about the expected result shapes what it can see.
  • Readable: every proposal is something a person can watch and name.
  • Statistically controlled: the list comes with an error guarantee, so it is not a list of noise.

2do-pilot

The five steps of Fig. 1, in order. Steps 2 and 3 are automatic and identical for every species and lab. Every design choice in them was made on public benchmarks, so our own experiments serve as one pre-registered test.

2.1Experiment

Step 1 · Scientist

The input is what a lab already has: videos from a randomized experiment, one or more per animal or group, with the treatment of each. The camera must be fixed. No annotation is required. If the lab already scores some behaviours, those scores can be supplied as extra units of the representation (§2.2): the search then conditions on them and reports only what the treatment changed beyond them.

2.2Behavioural representation

Step 2 · do-pilot

A frozen, general-purpose vision model encodes each frame. Only the tokens on the animals are kept, each paired with its change over the last seconds so that movement is visible. A sparse dictionary (sparse autoencoder) turns each token into a few active neurons. A neuron's value in a frame is its strongest activation on any animal. Each video is summarised per neuron by time active, bouts per minute and latency to the first bout.

Labels never shape the representation; they are used only afterwards, to score it. What shapes it are a few priors that hold in every domain:

  • Fixed viewpoint: one camera, one arena, so a change in the image is a change in the animals.
  • Sparsity: at any moment only a few behaviours are present.
  • Position and rotation invariance: a behaviour means the same thing anywhere in the arena, in any orientation.
  • Recurrence: a behaviour is a pattern that repeats across animals and videos.

2.3Interventional contrast

Step 3 · do-pilot

Which neurons did the treatment change? Testing each neuron alone fails: neurons are correlated, so one real effect lights up many neurons that only co-vary with it. We want the few neurons that carry the whole effect.

Definition · Neural Effect Sufficiency Search, NESS(ε)

Let T be the randomized treatment and Z ∈ ℝm a video's neuron summaries. A set S ⊆ {1,…,m} is ε-sufficient if

I( T ; Z−S | ZS ) ≤ ε

NESS(ε) returns an inclusion-minimal ε-sufficient set: a treatment classifier that sees only ZS loses at most ε nats of log-loss against one that sees every neuron.

  • Causal reading. Because T is randomized, any change the treatment causes in behaviour passes through ZS, up to an error that shrinks with ε bound to be verified.
  • Assumptions. Independent videos, a dictionary fixed before the analysis, and sample splitting. No causal graph, and no claim that neurons match true behavioural factors.
  • Algorithm. Add neurons by largest held-out information gain until the residual is below ε, drop any that became redundant, then certify sufficiency and each neuron's necessity on held-out data.

Why not NES. Our earlier method, Neural Effect Search, has no defined target set. It orders neurons by raw effect size under an assumption that does not hold in general, and it never removes a neuron once selected. On a semi-synthetic CelebA benchmark its precision falls from 0.80 to 0.49 as the sample grows from 250 to 5,000. Its claimed family-wise error control does not hold at a fixed test level.

2.4Hypotheses

Step 4 · Scientist

For each selected neuron, do-pilot shows the clips where it fires most and a random sample of clips where it fires at all, from both arms. The scientist names the behaviour and states a hypothesis: what changed, in which direction, and what else should follow if it is real. NESS also tags each neuron as an effect (it shifts with the treatment) or context (it is needed only to read another neuron's shift), and re-running without a neuron shows which effects it carried. Hypotheses the scientist already held join this list here and are treated the same way.

2.5Validation

Step 5 · Scientist

A neuron is a lead, not a result. Each hypothesis goes through the same five checks, on data the search never saw:

DefineWrite an operational definition of the named behaviour, so another person can score it.
Rule out nuisanceCheck the neuron against recording day, camera and batch before spending effort.
MeasureScore the behaviour on held-out videos or a new cohort, blind to treatment, by hand or by a trained annotator.
TestPre-registered estimate of the effect on the scored behaviour (prediction-powered when an AI annotator scores it).
InterpretPlace it in the field: mechanism, literature, and the next experiment.
