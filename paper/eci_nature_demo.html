Exploratory Causal Inference × Behavioural Science

Riccardo Cadei, Tommaso Mencattini, Francesco Locatello, et al. Draft of 16 Oct 2026 Results are placeholders until validated
Abstract

Randomized experiments are how behavioural science establishes cause and effect, yet what an experiment can reveal is fixed before it starts: researchers decide which behaviours to score, and any effect outside that list goes unseen. Video records far more than anyone annotates. Here we introduce do-pilot, an empiricist assistant that reads the raw videos of a randomized experiment and proposes which behaviours the treatment changed, before anyone decides what to look for. do-pilot learns, without labels, a dictionary of interpretable visual units (neurons) that each fire on one recognisable pattern of behaviour, and searches it with an interventional contrast that returns the smallest set of neurons carrying everything the treatment changed, with statistical error control. Each selected neuron is a hypothesis that a scientist can watch, name and test. Applied to three systems, fungal exposure in ant colonies, autism-risk genes in mice and motor-neuron transcription factors in Xenopus frogs, do-pilot proposes novel hypotheses in each, which we validate with targeted follow-up annotation and experiments, yielding new insights into social immunity, social behaviour and motor development. do-pilot turns existing video archives into a source of testable causal hypotheses.

TL;DR

do-pilot watches the videos of a randomized experiment, finds what the treatment changed without being told what to look for, and hands the scientist a short list of hypotheses to test. In ants, mice and frogs, each list led to a validated discovery.

1Motivation

Behavioural science runs on randomized experiments: a gene is knocked out, a colony is exposed to a pathogen, a drug is given, and the animals are filmed. The causal question is then answered by scoring a short, predefined list of behaviours (an ethogram). That list is the bottleneck. It is chosen before the data exist, it reflects what the field already expects, and scoring it by hand costs hours per hour of video. Anything the treatment changed outside the list stays invisible, even though it is sitting in the footage.

Machine learning has so far helped with the second half of this process. AI annotators score known behaviours faster, and prediction-powered causal inference makes their scores statistically valid. Both still answer only the questions someone already asked. The missing piece is the first half: deciding which questions to ask.

We build an empiricist assistant for that step. It does not theorise and it does not decide what is true. It reads the data of an experiment and proposes, with error control, a short list of observable changes the treatment caused. The scientist then names, tests and interprets them. To be trusted, such an assistant must meet three requirements:

  • Label-free. Nothing about the expected outcome may shape what it can see.
  • Readable. Every proposal must be something a person can watch and name.
  • Statistically controlled. Proposals must come with a guarantee, so a list of hypotheses is not a list of noise.

2do-pilot

do-pilot is one fixed pipeline, run unchanged on every species and lab setup. Its assumptions are written down as explicit priors before any data are analysed, and its design choices are made on public benchmarks only, so our own experiments serve as a single pre-registered test.

2.1Representation learning

Each frame is encoded by a frozen, general-purpose vision model. Only the tokens on the animals are kept, each paired with its change over the last second so that movement is visible. A sparse dictionary (sparse autoencoder) turns every token into a handful of active neurons. A neuron's value in a frame is its strongest activation on any animal; a video is summarised per neuron by time spent active, bouts per minute and latency to first bout. Labels never shape the representation. They are used only afterwards, to score how well neurons align with known behaviours.

2.2Interventional contrast

Given the dictionary, which neurons did the treatment change? Testing each neuron alone is not enough: neurons are correlated, so one real effect lights up many neurons that merely co-vary with it. We want the few neurons that carry the whole effect.

Definition · Neural Effect Sufficiency Search, NESS(ε)

Let T be the randomized treatment and Z ∈ ℝm a video's neuron summaries. A set S ⊆ {1,…,m} is ε-sufficient if

I( T ; Z−S | ZS ) ≤ ε

NESS(ε) returns an inclusion-minimal ε-sufficient set. In words: a treatment classifier that sees only ZS loses at most ε nats of log-loss against one that sees every neuron.

  • Why it is causal. Because T is randomized, any change in the distribution of behaviour that the treatment causes must pass through ZS, up to a bound that shrinks with ε (bound to be verified).
  • What it assumes. Independent videos, a dictionary fixed before the analysis, and sample splitting. No causal graph and no claim that neurons match true behavioural factors.
  • How it is found. Add neurons by largest held-out information gain until the residual is below ε, remove any that became redundant, then certify sufficiency and the necessity of each neuron on a held-out split.
  • What it returns. Each selected neuron is tagged effect (it shifts with T) or context (needed to read another neuron's shift). Removing a neuron and re-running shows which effects it carried or masked.

Why not NES. Our earlier method, Neural Effect Search (NES), has no defined target set, and orders neurons by raw effect size under an assumption that does not hold in general. It has no backward step, so redundant neurons are never removed: on a semi-synthetic CelebA benchmark its precision falls from 0.80 to 0.49 as the sample grows from 250 to 5,000. Its claimed family-wise error control does not hold at a fixed test level.
